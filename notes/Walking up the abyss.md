In the high-altitude Ladakh, walking has always been a main form of connectivity. Days and months of walking to reach farm fields, grazing grounds, trade centers, and to cross mtshams (meeting points), as a way of connecting, making sense of a place—also of one’s place among the mountains. 

Trekking is new and different; it is recreational, it is urban, or rather remoteness for the incessantly connected urban. One’s footsteps in quiet solitude on the untended Earth, left to its natural fate, and occasional calls of birds that sometimes respond to your calls, aware of your rare presence, getting rarer with altitude, are but a mark of yourself that will not remain any longer than you.

As one’s ration is spent, the body builds a quiet comfort with the weight of the backpack, being shared with the wilderness. Washing oneself is a major decision; water is either in shortage or in abundance, waste and leftovers turn into fuel, and sitting in dust becomes unbothersome. Conversations come from deeper within that the walker was not aware of; interactions become intense, and human connection is formed beyond the expectations accumulated through life, once the nature of place and mind is altered (Nan Shephard). 

All that is left is what is basic to one’s being.

Everything from this height is practically bottomless. One’s destination is the highest one can survive, and a failure is merely an extra day’s wait. But the next day comes, and off we go to climb that slippery slope or cross that glacial stream—forcing us down, pushing us away—in a test not of our civility but of our animality. 

Back hurts, shoes break, skin catches fire in a slow rhythm of the wilderness that one will remain unaware of, until the fire has burnt through.

Yet all this for what? Where are we walking to?

Walking on the mountains is like walking up an abyss, not a bottomless depth, but a roofless flight taken on our bare spirit. If one comes through, it is not because they have reached their destiny, but because they have started a journey into the voluminousness of the mountains. Even the compass is a failure—forcing our world onto a two-dimensional four-direction simulacrum that won’t make sense beyond a piece of paper. One finally comes to realize that the space is not experienced just in uniform distance but is built through an uncertain multidimensionality where surrendering means passing through. It is a contentment with not seeking complete knowledge (Macfarlane). This is a space that cannot be reached or discovered but can only be created. This is where one will not find victory but learn that all the walking happened next to one.

On one such journey, you will arrive, in an unknown location, in the company of people you have not chosen, and you will realize that all that walking was for that exact moment.
